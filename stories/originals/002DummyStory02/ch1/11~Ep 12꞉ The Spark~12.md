# **Episode 11:**

## *The Spark*

He woke on his platform high in the old hardwood, shivering as the freezing wind whipped through his thin cloak.

He stepped down onto the lashed timber floorboards.

STING.

A sharp, stabbing pain shot through his bare heel.

He yanked his foot up, uttering a harsh, clicking noise in his throat. A dry, brown seed pod was pinned to his skin—one of the clinging pods from weeks ago.

It had been hiding in the corner of his shelter, waiting.

He pulled the needles from his foot, grabbed several long wooden sticks, and used them to push the dry pods, leaves, and debris out over the edge into the dark gap below.

Clear the wood. Keep the floor clean.

It became a habit.

While clearing the platform near his stored gear, his eyes caught the rusted iron tool he had salvaged from the cave.

The edges were crumbling, coated in a thick, flaking crust that was slowly eating the metal away.

He picked up a heavy, pale stone and struck the blade hard to knock the flakes loose.

CLACK.

CLACK.

SPARK.

A bright, hot needle of light flashed from the impact.

It landed directly in the pile of dry leaf dust and sap-coated scrapings he had just pushed into the corner.

A faint, grey thread of smoke rose from the dust.

Then, a tiny, glowing red eye opened in the pile.

A yellow tongue of light flickered into existence.

He stared.

Captivated. Curious. Completely ignorant.

He reached out his hand, leaning in to touch the strange, glowing shape.

STING.

The heat bit his finger instantly, searing the skin with a sudden, violent bite.

He yanked his hand back with a sharp gasp, cradling his blistered knuckles against his chest.

The yellow light was born.

And it had already tried to eat him.

The pain burned, but the air around the small light was sweet, pushing back the deadening chill of the winter sky.

He feared it.

He respected it.

He knew that the yellow light consumed wood, so he refused to let it touch the timber floor.

He climbed down to the ruined courtyard, found a massive, flat stone slab, and hauled it up into the tree with straining muscles.

He placed the slab directly on his platform floor and built the yellow light on top of it.

Sensible. Contained.

He sat beside the flat stone, shivering, holding his numb hands near the heat.

He pulled a fresh strip of meat from his pack to eat.

His fingers were stiff from the cold.

The meat slipped.

It dropped straight into the glowing red center of the stone hearth.

His throat clicked in frustration.

He assumed it was lost, ruined by the black ash.

He left it there overnight.

The next morning, he poked at the cooled black dust and pulled the charred piece of meat out.

It smelled entirely different.

Rich. Heavy. Warm.

He took a cautious bite.

It was softer, rich with flavor, entirely unlike the tough, raw flesh he had forced down his throat for weeks.

The next time he hunted, he placed the meat near the glowing heat instead of inside it.

He crushed a few leaves of the sharp-smelling herb between two stones and rubbed them onto the flesh.

Warm meat was no longer an accident.

It was a choice.

Not all interactions with the heat went smoothly.

The world punished every oversight with disasters.

The first came from below.

He was sitting near the stone slab when he noticed a thin smoke rising not from the light itself, but from underneath the rock.

He pushed the stone aside.

The thick wooden platform floor beneath it was pitch black, smoldering with red-hot char.

The intense heat had traveled right through the stone, eating the wood underneath.

It did not just eat what it touched.

It ate from beneath.

The second came from the sky.

The winter wind shifted suddenly, blowing the heavy grey smoke straight into his enclosed tree shelter.

Within seconds, his platform became a choking trap.

His eyes burned and watered, blinding him.

He coughed violently, his lungs burning as he scrambled out onto the open branches, gasping for air.

The light needed a way out. The smoke needed a path.

The third came from his own attempt to save it.

A sudden freezing rain hit his tree, threatening to kill the small warmth.

Desperate, he grabbed a heavy branch dripping with wet rain and thick, sticky plant sap.

He threw it onto the stone slab.

The water on the wood hissed, smothering the yellow tongue into a choking cloud of steam.

Then the thick sap caught.

WHOOM.

A violent, roaring wave of orange flame exploded upward, singeing his hair and throwing hot sparks across his shelter.

He scrambled backward, falling onto his spine, breathing hard.

Wet wood smothers.

The sticky sap explodes.

The fuel mattered.

Down at the base of his tree, the world was quietly decaying.

Months of creature waste, wet leaves, discarded bones, and rain had rotted into a dark, foul pile beneath his platform.

He noticed the strange crusting on his metal again.

He decided to test a thought.

He took two identical scraps of rusted iron blade.

He buried one scrap deep inside the wet, foul soil at the base of the tree.

He placed the second scrap high above on a clean, dry rock.

He waited.

Days passed while he tended his hearth, cooked his food, and mapped the freezing winter landscape.

When he finally checked the scraps, the answer was clear.

The metal on the dry rock remained unchanged.

The metal buried in the wet earth was crumbling, eaten away by a thick, flaky white rot.

He held the ruined metal in his hand, looking down at the dark, wet soil.

He did not know what to call the damp pile.

He only knew one truth.

This earth eats metal.

He tucked his hands inside his heavy cloak, sitting beside his small, steady hearth as the snow drifted past the high branches.

The yellow light was contained.

His food was warm.

His mind was watchful.

Now, he was just waiting to see what else he could learn to tame.
