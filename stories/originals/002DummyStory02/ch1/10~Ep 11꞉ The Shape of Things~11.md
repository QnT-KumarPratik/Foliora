
# **Episode 11:**

## *The Shape of Things* 

Darkness.

Then light.

Then the weight of things that had no names.

He woke high in the branches, wrapped in the cold, damp musk of the roost.

The fabric clinging to his skin was heavy.

Foul.

Wrong.

He climbed down the thick bark, stripped the damp cloth away, and dropped it into the mud.

He washed it in the shallows of the grey lake.

Badly.

He hung it on a jagged stone and watched the wind move it.

Fabric was just a thing.

Things could be dropped.

Things could change.

He returned to the green shoots near the broken road.

He did not grab them this time.

He picked up a flat stone.

He pressed a single sharp-smelling leaf between the rock and the pavement.

The smell stayed.

Clean.

Cool.

He gathered a small bundle, rubbing the leaves against his skin to deaden the stench of the high branches.

A few broken stems slipped from his pack, falling unnoticed into the damp soil beneath his tree.

The days began to fold into one another, swallowed by a heavy, repeating rhythm.

From his platform high in the old hardwood, the world below ceased to be a chaos of disconnected walls and dead streets.

He looked out over the stone canopy, tracing the spine of the ancient city with his eyes.

The ruins were not random.

They were a shape.

He pulled out the notebook, pressing graphite to paper to draw what his eyes could see from above.

A square for the wide lake.

A line for the narrow stream.

A dark mark for the damp cave where the bones lay.

An intersecting grid for the stone avenues.

As he mapped the thin blue thread of the stream, he began following its course past the collapsed bridge.

The quiet trickling water grew wider, moving into a deeper channel with an increasingly strong current.

Eventually, it opened into a rushing river.

He stepped into the shallows to fill a vial, but the pull of the water hit his legs like a thrown log, sweeping his feet out from under him.

He slammed onto his back, clawing frantically at slippery river rocks until he dragged himself gasping onto the muddy bank.

Water was not always a quiet pool waiting for his hands.

Sometimes water was a jaw.

Up in the high boughs, he was not alone.

The winged, leathery-skinned creatures were still there, hanging upside down in the shadows of the upper canopy.

They were loud.

They were messy, dropping foul waste over his gear until his platform felt like a rotting pit.

Yet they did not bite.

They were just disgusting, chaotic roommates sharing the height.

At dusk, as they stretched their long finger-wings and dropped into the sky, they made high, sharp clicking noises in the back of their throats.

He sat in the dark, watching them, and tried to shape his own tongue.

He clicked.

A sharp, dry sound against his palate.

He tried again, attempting to match the rapid chatter of the small, darting things that ran through the bushes during the day.

His throat clicked and produced a strange, clumsy whistle that caught him off guard.

He could now try to echo the forest.

The animals moved below him like clockwork, yet their patterns were never permanent.

The spotted runners used the stream beds when the sun was high, but when the days grew cooler, they vanished deeper into the thick brush.

He saw the golden eyes three times in the shadows of the ruined balconies.

The being never charged.

It only watched.

He stopped trying to guess what they were.

He simply marked where they had been and kept moving.

Not everything was a lesson learned.

Sometimes a lesson was just pain.

A spear snapped against a granite pillar, leaving him with a broken shaft.

A sudden rain washed his dried meat into grey, unpalatable mash.

A clumsy foot slipped on a wet branch, dropping him several feet into a thicket of thorn bushes.

He bled.

He clicked his tongue in frustration.

Below his platform, the ground grew thick with waste.

Fallen leaves, snapped branches, discarded bones, and layers of wet guano accumulated in the damp shade beneath the tree.

Then the rain came, heavy and freezing, soaking the pile into a dark, heavy mass.

Then the frost arrived.

The green leaves turned brittle and fell.

Cold mornings turned the bark slick with ice.

Climbing the tree became a nightmare of frozen fingers and slipping boots.

During one ascent, his numb hands lost their grip entirely, and he plunged sideways, crashing through the lower boughs before landing hard in a deep drift of early snow.

He lay there, buried to his chest, coughing icy powder into the quiet air.

The high tree was no longer a sanctuary; the biting wind turned his elevated platform into an open freezer.

Warmth was no longer just a comfort.

It was a resource he was running out of.

And down in the snow, the tracks of the heavy, four-legged stalkers had grown sparse, disappearing entirely into the white silence.

He survived not by becoming strong, but by making fewer stupid mistakes.

He learned to test a frosted branch before putting his full weight on it.

He learned to wrap his feet in thick layers of dried bark to keep the cold from deadening his toes.

One morning, walking past the base of his hardwood tree, he noticed something strange pushing up through the melting frost.

Small green shoots.

He crouched, crushing a stem between his fingers, and sniffed.

The sharp, cool smell of the mint-leaf plant.

He looked up at his platform, then back down at the soil.

The fragments he had dropped weeks ago had rooted themselves in the dirt.

Plant a piece of a thing, and the thing returns.

He took a few snapped twigs of the herb and deliberately pushed them into the damp earth nearby.

By the time the snow settled deep over the ruins, his notebook was heavy with charcoal lines.

Detailed maps filled the pages—rivers, dead ends, safe climbs, and hunting grounds.

He sat beneath his shelter, pulling his rusted iron tools from his pack to inspect them.

He froze.

The metal blades were changing.

A strange, flaky crust had formed along the iron edges, eating into the metal.

Something was destroying his tools.

He scraped at the crumbling edge with his thumb, unaware of whatever was eating at it.

He closed the notebook, tucked his calloused hands beneath his cloak, and looked out over the white, frozen stones of the city.

He had stopped asking if he would survive the winter.

Now, he was just watching to see what the cold would reveal next.
